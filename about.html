Routes on this map are generated automatically from OpenStreetMap roads and trails and elevation data. Nobody has checked them on the ground. They are not route recommendations.

Before you go

  • Confirm access. A route or road can cross private land, tribal land, military land, or areas that are closed. A mapped road may be gated, washed out, or legal only with a permit. Check with the land manager (Forest Service, BLM, Park Service, state) for closures, permits, and current conditions. Turn on Ownership (next to Map) to see who manages the land, or use the Land filter to show peaks by who manages the summit; both are approximate.
  • Proceed at your own risk. Using these routes is entirely your own decision and responsibility; the author makes no guarantee that any route is safe, legal, open, or accurate.
  • Off-trail legs are straight lines. They ignore cliffs, passes, rivers, and brush. The real way up can be longer, harder, or not possible.
  • Mapped trails may not be for hiking. Mapped trails can be climbers' routes, abandoned trails, or mapping errors.
  • Road grades come from map tags and can be out of date. Google Maps directions may not route the last unpaved stretch.
  • Distance, gain, and time are rough estimates. Weather, snow, fitness, and route-finding change everything. You are responsible for your own safety and judgment.

What's on the map

  • Peaks: every summit with at least 250′ of prominence and 1,000′ of elevation in the lower 48 and Hawaii, plus a few one-offs such as Boundary Peak (242′ prominence).
  • Routes: computed for peaks with at least 250′ of prominence that are at least 4,000′ high, or 3,000′ in the states east of the Mississippi.

How routes are made

  • Route: the shortest mapped trail path, then a straight off-trail line to the summit, counted at 1.3× its length (shown dotted).
  • Choices: up to six different approaches are found, and the three with the shortest estimated round-trip time are shown. Different trailheads come first, plus different ways up from the same trailhead.
  • Time: 2.5 mph on trail, 1 mph off-trail, plus 1 hour per 2,000′ of gain, with the descent taking 70% of the ascent time.
  • Drive road: graded paved / graded dirt / high clearance / 4WD from OpenStreetMap tags; routes needing high clearance or 4WD are labeled.

Your log (My Peaks)

  • Needs Chrome or Edge on a computer. My Peaks saves your log to a folder on your computer, and only those browsers let a web page do that. In Safari, Firefox, or on a phone you can use the map and routes, but not the log.
  • What you can record: mark a peak Want to, or log a climb or attempt. Each peak can have as many ascents as you like, each with a date, notes, photos, and a GPX track. Tracks are drawn on the map. Peaks get a Skeleton Score (0–10).
  • Seeing your peaks: logged peaks are colored on the map (climbed, attempted, want to). "Only my peaks" hides everything else, and the peak list can show just your peaks.
  • Where it's saved: Choose folder picks a folder on your computer. Your log is saved there as ordinary files: my_peaks.json, with your photos and GPX tracks in my_peaks_photos and my_peaks_gpx. There's no account, and nothing is uploaded.
  • Keep it safe: put the folder in iCloud Drive, OneDrive, or Dropbox to back it up and to use your log on another computer. On a later visit Chrome may ask you to Reconnect the folder; choosing "Allow on every visit" skips that.
